\begin{abstract}
We derive the coordinate Pythagorean identity from a primitive length function,
axial calibration, mirror symmetry, and a transitive rigid one-parameter
family of linear planar motions containing an oriented quarter-turn.
The matrix of the quarter-turn and the normal form of the family are
derived from algebraic hypotheses. Rigidity then implies a determinant-one
identity, and rotating a vector onto the horizontal axis yields
$a^2+b^2=c^2$, without taking the Cartesian diagonal-distance formula
as a premise. The main proof is group-theoretic and does not require
differentiability. The original Lie-theoretic treatment, using the generator
$J$ and classification of invariant quadratic forms, is retained as a
supplementary derivation. The manuscript is AI-generated; the motivating
request to seek an original proof is not itself evidence of historical novelty.
\end{abstract}

Many familiar proofs of the Pythagorean theorem use geometric dissection, similarity, or trigonometric/algebraic identities. When extending the theorem to analytic geometry, standard presentations frequently define the Cartesian metric distance via $d(P_1,P_2)=\sqrt{\Delta x^2+\Delta y^2}$, introducing potential circularity if used to evaluate diagonal segment lengths.

A subsequent Lie-theoretic specialization studies the infinitesimal generator and its invariant quadratic forms. Both routes are conditional results and neither defines the primitive length by a Euclidean square root.


\section{Primary axiomatic and group-theoretic derivation}
\label{sec:primary}
Let $L:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R$ be a primitive geometric length function,
not defined by $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. We state the exact hypotheses used
for the principal result:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Axial calibration:} $L(z,0)=|z|$ for every real $z$.
\item \textbf{Mirror symmetry:} $L(x,-y)=L(x,y)$ for all $x,y$.
\item \textbf{Primitive oriented quarter-turn:} a linear map $Q$ obeys
$Q(1,0)=(0,1)$ and $Q^2=-I$.
\item \textbf{Linear group action:} a family of linear maps
$F_t$ satisfies $F_{s+t}=F_s\circ F_t$ for all $s,t\in\mathbb R$,
and some $F_\theta$ equals $Q$.
\item \textbf{Rigidity:} $L(F_t(x,y))=L(x,y)$ for every $t,x,y$.
\item \textbf{Axis reachability:} for the chosen vector $(a,b)$,
there exist $T\in\mathbb R$ and $z\geq0$ with
$F_T(a,b)=(z,0)$.
\end{enumerate}
The last two conditions are geometric assumptions, not conclusions
of linearity alone. The main proof does not assume differentiability.

\begin{lemma}[The metric-free quarter-turn normal form]
The quarter-turn satisfies $Q(x,y)=(-y,x)$.
Every $F_t$ has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:rotational-normal-form}
 F_t(x,y)=\bigl(u(t)x-v(t)y,\;v(t)x+u(t)y\bigr)
\end{equation}
for suitable real functions $u,v$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The square relation gives $Q(0,1)=Q^2(1,0)=(-1,0)$.
Linearity yields $Q(x,y)=(-y,x)$. Since
$F_t Q=F_{t+\theta}=F_{\theta+t}=QF_t$,
the arbitrary matrix
$F_t=\begin{pmatrix}p&q\\r&s\end{pmatrix}$
commutes with
$J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
Thus $q=-r$ and $s=p$. Taking $u(t)=p$,
$v(t)=r$ proves~\eqref{eq:rotational-normal-form}.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Rigidity eliminates radial scaling]\label{lem:unit}
For every $t$, the coefficients in~\eqref{eq:rotational-normal-form}
satisfy $u(t)^2+v(t)^2=1$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Fix $t$ and abbreviate $u=u(t)$, $v=v(t)$.
Rigidity and axial calibration imply
$L(u,v)=L(F_t(1,0))=L(1,0)=1$.
Reflection symmetry gives $L(u,-v)=1$.
But~\eqref{eq:rotational-normal-form} gives
$F_t(u,-v)=(u^2+v^2,0)$. Hence
\[
 |u^2+v^2|=L(u^2+v^2,0)=L(u,-v)=1.
\]
As the sum of two squares is nonnegative, it equals $1$.
No quadratic distance formula was assumed for $L$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Group-theoretic coordinate Pythagorean theorem]
\label{thm:primary}
Under the six stated hypotheses, let $c=L(a,b)$.
Then $a^2+b^2=c^2$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose $T,z$ with $z\geq0$ and $F_T(a,b)=(z,0)$.
Let $u=u(T)$, $v=v(T)$. The normal form yields
\[
 ua-vb=z,\qquad va+ub=0,
\]
and Lemma~\ref{lem:unit} gives $u^2+v^2=1$.
By a direct algebraic expansion,
\[
 (ua-vb)^2+(va+ub)^2
 =(u^2+v^2)(a^2+b^2)=a^2+b^2.
\]
The left-hand side is $z^2$. Independently,
rigidity and axial calibration identify
\[
 c=L(a,b)=L(F_T(a,b))=L(z,0)=z.
\]
Consequently $a^2+b^2=z^2=c^2$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[What is and is not assumed]
The coordinate form of a quarter-turn, the normal form of
$F_t$, and the determinant-one identity are derived, not
postulated. However, linearity, the half-turn property,
presence of the quarter-turn in an abelian one-parameter
action, mirror symmetry, rigidity, and reachability are
substantive hypotheses. Lean verifies this implication,
not the independence or minimality of these axioms.
\end{remark}

\section{Lie-theoretic specialization: the original approach}
We formalize the relationship between primitive Euclidean geometry and Cartesian coordinates through two axioms.

\begin{axiom}[Orthogonal Coordinate Calibration]
The two coordinate directions correspond to perpendicular geometric directions. Furthermore, any two points in $\mathbb{R}^2$ whose coordinate difference vector is $(L,0)$ or $(0,L)$ have a primitive Euclidean geometric separation equal to $|L|$.
\end{axiom}

\begin{axiom}[Rigid Rotational Congruence and Transitivity]
Continuous rotation about the origin $C(0,0)$ is a length-preserving rigid motion belonging to the one-parameter flow group
\begin{equation}\label{eq:flow}
G=\{R(t)=e^{tJ}:t\in\mathbb{R}\},\qquad J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Furthermore, for any two rays emanating from $C$, there exists a transformation $R(T)\in G$ mapping one ray onto the other.
\end{axiom}

\subsection{Classification of invariant quadratic forms under \texorpdfstring{$G$}{G}}
We establish that the infinitesimal generator $J$ forces the form of the Euclidean quadratic invariant up to an overall scalar multiplier.

\begin{lemma}[Classification of Nonzero Symmetric Quadratic Invariants]
Let $Q(\mathbf r)=\mathbf r^{T}M\mathbf r$ be a nonzero symmetric quadratic form generated by $M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\\beta&\gamma\end{pmatrix}$. Then $Q$ is invariant under the rotational flow $G$ if and only if $M=\lambda I$ for some scalar $\lambda\ne0$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
By definition, $Q$ is invariant under $R(t)=e^{tJ}$ if and only if $R(t)^TMR(t)=M$ for all $t\in\mathbb{R}$. Differentiating at $t=0$ yields the infinitesimal condition
\begin{equation}\label{eq:infinitesimal}J^TM+MJ=0.\end{equation}
\textbf{Uniqueness.} Since $J^T=-J$, equation~\eqref{eq:infinitesimal} reduces to $MJ=JM$. Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:products}
MJ=\begin{pmatrix}\beta&-\alpha\\\gamma&-\beta\end{pmatrix},\qquad
JM=\begin{pmatrix}-\beta&-\gamma\\\alpha&\beta\end{pmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Equating corresponding entries forces $\beta=0$ and $\alpha=\gamma$. Setting $\alpha=\gamma=\lambda$ gives $M=\lambda I$. Since $Q\ne0$, we have $\lambda\ne0$.

\textbf{Existence.} Conversely, suppose $M=\lambda I$ with $\lambda\ne0$. Then
\begin{equation}
J^TM+MJ=\lambda(J^T+J)=\lambda(-J+J)=0.
\end{equation}
Differentiating $Q(\mathbf r(t))$ along any integral curve $\dot{\mathbf r}=J\mathbf r$ yields
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dt}Q(\mathbf r(t))=\mathbf r^T(J^TM+MJ)\mathbf r=0.
\end{equation}
Thus every quadratic form $Q(x,y)=\lambda(x^2+y^2)$ with $\lambda\ne0$ is conserved along the flow $G$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
The identities $R(t)^TR(t)=I$ and $\det R(t)=1$ follow from $J^T=-J$ and $\operatorname{tr}J=0$. Thus $G$ is a one-parameter subgroup of $SO(2)$ generated by $J$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{The original dynamical and congruence proof}
\begin{theorem}[Coordinate Pythagorean Theorem]
In a calibrated orthogonal coordinate frame with primitive length congruence, the length-preserving flow $e^{tJ}$, and ray transitivity, let $\triangle CAB$ be a right triangle with leg lengths $|CA|=a$ and $|AB|=b$, and hypotenuse length $|CB|=c$. Then
\[
a^2+b^2=c^2.
\]
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Choose the origin at $C$, the positive $x$-axis along $CA$, and the positive $y$-axis parallel to $AB$.

By the coordinate-calibration axiom, the coordinate axes represent orthogonal geometric directions. Because $|CA|=a$, vertex $A$ has coordinates $(a,0)$. Because $AB$ is parallel to the $y$-axis with $|AB|=b$, vertex $B$ has coordinates $(a,b)$. The hypotenuse $CB$ has primitive geometric length $c$.

By the rigid-congruence and transitivity axiom, there exists $R(T)=e^{TJ}\in G$ that maps the ray $CB$ onto the positive $x$-axis, placing $B(a,b)$ at $B'(r,0)$ with $r>0$. We evaluate the terminal axial coordinate $r$ by two channels.

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Channel 1: Dynamical conservation.}
By the invariant-quadratic-form lemma, $Q(x,y)=\lambda(x^2+y^2)$ ($\lambda\ne0$) is conserved along the trajectory from $(a,b)$ to $(r,0)$:
\begin{equation}
Q(a,b)=Q(r,0)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lambda(a^2+b^2)=\lambda(r^2+0^2).
\end{equation}
Dividing by $\lambda\ne0$ gives
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qchannel} a^2+b^2=r^2.\end{equation}

\medskip\noindent\textbf{Channel 2: Geometric congruence.}
By the rigid-congruence and transitivity axiom, rigid rotation preserves primitive geometric length, so $|CB'|=|CB|=c$. By the coordinate-calibration axiom, the segment from $(0,0)$ to $(r,0)$ has geometric length $r$. Therefore
\begin{equation}\label{eq:congruence}r=c.\end{equation}
Combining equations~\eqref{eq:qchannel} and~\eqref{eq:congruence} yields
\begin{equation}a^2+b^2=c^2.\end{equation}
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Foundational Scope]
This derivation proves the Pythagorean theorem relative to an underlying Euclidean congruence structure and a specified rotational flow, rather than deriving metric geometry from purely algebraic primitives. Its contribution lies in showing that once continuous rotational flow and primitive length congruence are logically decoupled from coordinate metrics, rotational symmetry forces the $x^2+y^2$ quadratic form up to scale, while congruence identifies its terminal axial coordinate with primitive geometric length without assuming the Cartesian distance formula.
\end{remark}

\section{Conclusion}
The coordinate Pythagorean identity follows algebraically from a primitive length function, rotational group structure and rigid congruence without assuming an a priori Cartesian diagonal-distance formula. The infinitesimal Lie-theoretic method remains valuable for understanding the generator and invariant quadratic forms, but is not required for the principal proof. Both formulations remain conditional on their explicitly stated geometric hypotheses.
